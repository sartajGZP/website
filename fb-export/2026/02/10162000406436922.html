Forgotten Chapters:

They hired teenage boys to connect phone calls. The boys cursed at customers, disconnected calls as pranks, and sometimes just didn't show up. So they fired all the boys—and hired women they could control, surveil, and overwork until some killed themselves.
This is the real story of telephone operators. Not a heartwarming tale of women's employment, but a brutal example of how corporations exploit gendered expectations to create compliant, controllable workforces.
In 1877, George W. Coy attended a lecture by Alexander Graham Bell demonstrating his new invention—the telephone. Coy, a Civil War veteran working in the telegraph industry, immediately saw the commercial potential.
But early telephones couldn't directly connect to each other. They needed a central switchboard where an operator could manually plug wires to connect one caller to another. Someone had to physically make every connection.
In 1878, Coy opened the first telephone exchange in New Haven, Connecticut, serving 21 subscribers. He needed operators to work the switchboard, and he did what was standard practice—he hired teenage boys.
This made sense at the time. Telegraph offices employed boys as messengers and operators. Boys were cheap labor—paid less than adult men, without the expectation of career advancement or benefits.
But the boys were terrible at the job.
They were rude to customers. They disconnected calls deliberately as jokes. They got into physical fights with each other at the switchboard. They showed up late or not at all. They cursed at callers who annoyed them.
The boys treated the work as temporary, unimportant, beneath them—because in their minds, it probably was. This was "women's work" essentially, requiring patience, politeness, service to others. Skills boys were neither trained in nor valued for developing.
Telephone companies quickly realized they had a problem. Their business model required courteous, reliable, attentive service. The teenage boys were providing none of these things.
So the companies made a calculated decision: replace the boys with girls and young women.
This wasn't about recognizing women's capabilities or creating employment opportunities. This was about finding workers who could be controlled, who society had trained to be deferential and service-oriented, who would accept lower wages and stricter oversight.
The first women operators were hired in the early 1880s. By 1910, there were 88,000 female telephone operators in the United States. By 1930, that number had nearly tripled to 235,000.
These weren't just jobs—they were grueling, surveilled, emotionally exhausting positions with impossible expectations.
Operators worked nine-hour shifts (sometimes longer without additional pay). They sat in rows at massive switchboards, elbow-to-elbow with dozens of other operators, wearing heavy headsets, making hundreds of connections per shift.
At busy exchanges, operators wore roller skates to move quickly between different sections of the switchboard. Think about that—the work was so physically demanding and the pace so frantic that skating was necessary.
The dress code was strict: long black dresses, no jewelry, hair pulled back. The companies wanted operators to be invisible, interchangeable, professional automatons.
And the rules were endless. No talking to other operators during shifts—not even in whispers. No smiling. Laughing was "the height of recklessness" and could result in discipline.
Every interaction was scripted. Operators had to say specific phrases in specific ways. They had to remain calm and polite no matter how customers treated them.
And their performance was constantly monitored. Supervisors used "listening boards"—devices that allowed management to secretly listen to operators' calls without their knowledge.
Imagine working under those conditions. Every word you speak is monitored. You can't talk to your coworkers. You can't express any emotion. You must be perfectly polite to people who are often rude, impatient, or abusive. And you never know when you're being spied on.
This wasn't just strict management. This was dehumanizing surveillance and control.
The stress was unbearable. In 1899, Anna Byrne, a 25-year-old San Francisco operator, killed herself. At the inquest, the coroner made a remarkable statement:
"I firmly believe that the espionage to which telephone girls are constantly subjected drives them to suicidal desperation. They are overworked; and no mercy is shown them when a slight offense is committed by a trivial infraction of the company's rules."
A coroner officially blamed a telephone company for driving an employee to suicide through abusive working conditions.
Other operators agreed. One veteran told the San Francisco Examiner: "The wonder is that more telephone girls don't kill themselves. We are not allowed to speak even in a whisper to each other the nine hours we are on duty, much less smile, and to laugh out loud is the height of recklessness."
She described being forced to work 10 extra hours without pay—as punishment for giggling once during her shift.
Think about the psychology behind this management approach. The companies deliberately created conditions of isolation, surveillance, and emotional suppression. They punished any expression of humanity—talking, smiling, laughing—as infractions worthy of discipline.
They weren't managing employees. They were attempting to create human machines—emotionless, obedient, perfectly predictable.
And they specifically targeted women for this treatment because cultural expectations made women more vulnerable to this type of control.
Women were raised to be polite, deferential, service-oriented. They were trained to suppress their own needs and emotions to care for others. They were socialized to accept surveillance and control from authority figures.
The telephone companies exploited these gendered expectations ruthlessly. They wanted workers who wouldn't fight back, who would accept impossible conditions, who could be paid less than men.
When they hired boys, the boys rebelled—through rudeness, through refusing to take the work seriously, through physical resistance. Boys had been socialized to assert themselves, to resist authority, to value their own dignity over service to others.
When they hired women, the companies got compliance—not because women were naturally more suited to the work, but because women had been systematically trained to endure mistreatment without fighting back.
But women did fight back. Just differently.
In 1919, 8,000 New England Telephone Company operators went on strike, demanding better wages, shorter hours, and improved working conditions.
The strike was part of a broader labor movement, and it was partially successful—operators won some concessions, though not all their demands.
During World War I, female telephone operators served overseas in France, connecting military communications under dangerous conditions. These "Hello Girls" were essential to military operations, yet they weren't officially recognized as military personnel until decades later.
Their service—and the visible contribution women were making to the war effort—helped build support for women's suffrage. The 19th Amendment was ratified in 1920, and telephone operators' wartime contributions were part of the argument for why women deserved the vote.
So women telephone operators did resist, did fight for their rights, did achieve some victories. But always within a system designed to exploit them.
Then, in the 1930s, technology changed everything. Direct-dial technology allowed people to call each other without operator assistance.
And telephone companies immediately used this as an excuse to fire thousands of operators.
By 1940, the number of operators had dropped from 235,000 to under 200,000. The decline continued for decades.
This reveals another truth about women's employment in the early 20th century: women were hired not because companies valued their labor, but because women could be exploited more easily than men.
When teenage boys proved difficult to control, companies hired women because women could be surveilled, disciplined, and overworked more easily.
When technology made operators less necessary, companies had no loyalty to the women who'd built the telephone industry. They just fired them en masse.
The operators weren't valued employees. They were interchangeable labor—kept around only as long as they were profitable and controllable.
Today, there are fewer than 5,000 workers classified as telephone operators in the United States. The Bureau of Labor Statistics expects this number to decline by 20% by 2029.
The job that once employed a quarter million women has almost completely disappeared.
This isn't just a story about technological change. It's a story about how women's labor has been systematically exploited throughout history.
Telephone operators were:

Hired because they could be controlled more easily than men
Paid less than men for the same work
Subjected to dehumanizing surveillance and discipline
Worked under conditions so abusive that some killed themselves
Fired en masse when technology made them less necessary
Never adequately compensated or recognized for building an entire industry

This pattern repeats throughout history. Women are brought into industries when labor is needed and men are unavailable or difficult to control. Women are paid less, treated worse, and given fewer opportunities for advancement.
Then, when technology changes or economic conditions shift, women are the first fired. Their labor is treated as disposable, their contributions minimized or forgotten.
We romanticize telephone operators—the friendly voices connecting calls, the helpful "Hello Girls." But the reality was brutal exploitation disguised as employment opportunity.
The next time someone talks about how women "entered the workforce" in the early 20th century as if it was progress, remember the telephone operators.
Remember that they were hired to replace boys who were too difficult to control. Remember that they worked under constant surveillance, forbidden to smile or whisper to coworkers. Remember that some killed themselves from the stress. Remember that they were fired by the thousands when technology made them less profitable.
That's not progress. That's exploitation finding the most vulnerable workers and squeezing every possible drop of labor from them before discarding them.
The telephone operators built an industry. They made modern communication possible. They endured abuse, surveillance, and impossible working conditions.
And they've been almost completely forgotten—reduced to a nostalgic image of women in long dresses saying "Number, please?"
They deserved better. They deserve to be remembered for what they actually experienced, not the sanitized version history prefers.
Telephone operators: hired because women could be controlled. Overworked until some died. Fired when technology made them unnecessary.
That's the real story. Uncomfortable, ugly, but true.
And it's a pattern that continues today—in gig economy jobs, in service industries, in any work where corporations can find vulnerable workers to exploit until they're no longer profitable.
The technology changes. The exploitation remains.
Remember the telephone operators. Remember what was done to them. And fight to make sure it doesn't keep happening.
