Why are Yemen's gun laws exactly the same as the US gun laws (anyone can own a gun without a permit)?

While Yemen and the U.S. share the rare distinction of treating gun ownership as a right rather than a privilege, their laws are actually structured quite differently. The "permitless" similarity is more about the outcome than the law.
Here is a proper explanation of the legal, cultural, and practical differences.

1. The Legal Foundation (De Jure)
The "rights" come from two very different legal traditions:

United States: The right is Constitutional. The Second Amendment ("...the right of the people to keep and bear arms, shall not be infringed") acts as a check on the federal government. It is a legal shield that makes it very difficult for the government to ban broad categories of weapons or owners.

Yemen: The right is Statutory. Under Law No. 40 of 1992, Article 9 explicitly grants citizens the right to own rifles, machine guns, and revolvers for "legitimate defense." Unlike the U.S., where the right is "inherited" from common law, Yemen's government granted this right via a specific 1992 act to align with tribal customs.

2. Permits and Licensing

In the U.S., "no permit" usually refers to possession or carrying (in about 29 states), but there is a rigid federal process for purchasing.
The U.S. Reality: To buy from a dealer, you must pass a background check (NICS). You cannot legally buy a new Glock in the U.S. without "asking" the government for permission via that background check.

The Yemen Reality: In practice, there is no background check system. While the 1992 law suggests retailers should keep records, the law does not require a "permit to purchase." You simply go to a market (like the famous Suq al-Talh) and buy what you can afford.

3. Type of Weapons (The "Automatic" Divide)

This is where the laws diverge most sharply.
In the U.S.: Fully automatic weapons (machine guns) are heavily restricted by the National Firearms Act (1934). You can only buy one made before 1986, you must pay a $200 tax, and wait months for FBI approval.

In Yemen: The 1992 law explicitly includes machine guns as a right for personal use. In Yemen, an AK-47 (fully automatic) is considered a standard "personal" weapon, whereas in the U.S., it is a highly regulated, five-figure luxury item for collectors.

4. Why they look the same: "Tribalism vs. Constitutionalism"
The reason both countries have "permitless" cultures is due to a historical lack of centralized state monopoly on force:

Yemen's Tribal System: For centuries, Yemeni tribes have functioned as their own mini-governments. If a central government tried to require permits for guns, the tribes would see it as a declaration of war. The 1992 law was essentially the government "surrendering" to a reality they couldn't change.

The U.S. Frontier/Militia History: The U.S. began with a deep distrust of standing armies. The idea was that the "militia" (the people) should be armed so they wouldn't need a massive, potentially tyrannical government army.

5. The "War" Factor
Finally, there is the issue of Enforcement. Since the start of the Yemeni Civil War in 2014, "law" in Yemen is largely theoretical.
In Sanaa (Houthi-controlled), there are strict rules against carrying guns in the streets to maintain order.

In Tribal areas, there is no law but tribal law.
In the U.S., regardless of your right to own a gun, if you sell one to a felon or buy a machine gun without a stamp, the federal government (ATF) has the infrastructure to track, arrest, and prosecute you.
