In the U.S., "no permit" usually refers to possession or carrying (in about 29 states), but there is a rigid federal process for purchasing.

In 1877, George W. Coy attended a lecture by Alexander Graham Bell demonstrating his new invention—the telephone. Coy, a Civil War veteran working in the telegraph industry, immediately saw the commercial potential.
But early telephones couldn't directly connect to each other. They needed a central switchboard where an operator could manually plug wires to connect one caller to another. Someone had to physically make every connection.
In 1878, Coy opened the first telephone exchange in New Haven, Connecticut, serving 21 subscribers. He needed operators to work the switchboard, and he did what was standard practice—he hired teenage boys.
This made sense at the time. Telegraph offices employed boys as messengers and operators. Boys were cheap labor—paid less than adult men, without the expectation of career advancement or benefits.
