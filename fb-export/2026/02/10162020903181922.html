In 416 BCE, during the long and brutal Peloponnesian War, Athens turned its attention to a small island in the Aegean Sea.

The island was Melos, a Spartan colony that had tried to remain neutral in the conflict between Athens and Sparta.

By that point, Athens had built a powerful maritime empire. Tribute flowed in from allied states, and the Athenian Assembly held ultimate authority over war and policy.

When Melos refused to join the Athenian alliance or pay tribute, Athens sent envoys demanding submission.

The historian Thucydides recorded the exchange in what is now called the Melian Dialogue. In his account, the Athenians did not claim moral right or divine favor.

Instead, they argued openly that power determined justice. The strong do what they can, they told the Melians, and the weak suffer what they must.

Melos refused to surrender. After a siege, the city fell. The Athenian Assembly then voted on its fate. According to Thucydides, all adult male citizens of Melos were executed.

The women and children were sold into slavery. The island was repopulated with Athenian settlers.

The decision came not from a tyrant, but from a vote of citizens. The same democratic system that allowed public vote.
