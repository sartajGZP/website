Interesting Things Around World:

Yes, handwriting still matters — and the brain science behind it is fascinating.

In a study from the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, researchers used a 256-channel EEG system to monitor brain activity while university students wrote words by hand or typed them on a keyboard.

The difference was striking.

When students wrote by hand, their brains showed far more widespread connectivity, especially in regions linked with movement, sensory processing, attention, and memory formation. These patterns appeared mainly in theta and alpha brain waves — frequencies often associated with learning and encoding new information.

Typing, by contrast, produced much simpler brain activity. Pressing keys requires less fine motor control than shaping letters by hand, which may explain why the brain is less broadly engaged during keyboard use.

The finding does not mean typing is useless. Digital tools are fast, practical, and essential in modern education. But it does suggest that replacing handwriting completely may remove an important form of brain training.

Writing by hand forces the brain to coordinate vision, touch, movement, timing, and memory at once. That physical effort may be one reason handwritten notes often feel more connected to understanding.

In a world full of screens, the old pen-and-paper method may still be one of the best tools for deep learning.

Source: Van der Weel, F. R. R., & Van der Meer, A. L. H. (2024). Handwriting but not typewriting leads to widespread brain connectivity: A high-density EEG study with implications for the classroom. Frontiers in Psychology.
