In the cities he visited, human waste was not flushed away. It was collected, bought, and sold. A resource too valuable to lose.
F. H. King was an American soil scientist, a professor at Wisconsin and once chief of soil management at the USDA. He even designed the round silo you still see on farms today. By 1909 he was worried. American farms were burning through their soil, while half a world away farmers had kept theirs fertile for forty centuries.
So he spent nine months walking the fields of China, Korea and Japan, filling notebooks. What he saw was a closed loop with no waste in it. Crop stalks, animal manure, canal mud, and night soil all went back to the land that produced them. Nothing was thrown away, because nothing was considered worthless.
He died in 1911 before he could finish the book. His wife, Carrie, completed the final chapter and published it that year. Farmers of Forty Centuries would help to shape the organic farming movement for the next hundred years.
He crossed an ocean to learn what those farmers already knew in their bones. Take from the soil, and you have to give it back.
