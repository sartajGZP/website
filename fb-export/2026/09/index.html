🎬 HANUMAN ANSH — CREATING THE LOOK OF AN ERA

It was a privilege to be associated with peoples blockbuster Hanuman Ansh as the Senior DI Colourist.

Being a period film, Hanuman Ansh required a visual treatment that could take the audience beyond the present and into the world of another era.

The objective of the Color Grading was not to make the images simply look “old”, but to create a period-inspired cinematic aesthetic that felt authentic to the film’s world.

We developed the look by carefully working with colour palette, contrast, saturation, skin tones, highlights and shadows, creating a more organic, textured and timeless visual character.

The colour treatment was designed to complement the period setting — retaining earthy and natural tones, controlling modern-looking colours, shaping the contrast and highlights, and creating a visual texture that supports the atmosphere of the era.

At the same time, it was important to maintain the cinematic quality and clarity expected from a contemporary theatrical presentation. The challenge was to find the right balance between historical atmosphere and modern cinematic storytelling.

For me, the most rewarding part of the process was seeing the images gradually transform during the DI — from individual shots into a complete visual world belonging to another time.

Today, seeing Hanuman Ansh achieve blockbuster success and connect with audiences makes being a part of this journey even more special.

My gratitude to the entire team — Director- Vishal Chaturvedi, DOP - Vishal Bawa, Editor - Shankh, Producers and the creative & post-production departments — for their trust and collaboration.

Simulate it now:

A doorway is not measured in metres. It is measured in meetings.

Two streams sharing one opening spend the same strip of floor on a pair of people facing each other. Each head-on pair is a body-width that cannot be used by anyone else: one person waits, the other waits, and the gap is occupied twice for a single crossing. That is counterflow interference. Throughput is not “width times walking speed.” It is leftover width after opposing occupancy is subtracted. A wide two-way hole can carry less than the same total width cut into two one-way halves, because the mixed hole keeps paying that meeting tax.

Splitting the opening alone does nothing. Two mixed doors are just two smaller knots: same collision, same stop-and-sidestep, same queues backing into both rooms. The control is the split. The thing that works is direction. Assign each half a single heading and opposing occupancy in the mouth falls toward zero. The knot dissolves, contact stress drops, and the same metres start moving people instead of holding them.

That is why stations, stadiums, and Friday prayer doors mark enter and exit. Not manners. Capacity.

How Private Banking Exploded in India After a President Died

In December 1994, President Gyani Zail Singh Singh was severely injured when his car collided with a truck in Punjab on 29 November 1994. He was admitted to PGI Chandigarh where he died on 25 December 1994. The Centre declared an unprecedented 7 days of national mourning.

As soon as news broke that Singh was in ICU, Government, PSU and bank employees in Delhi started planning their big holiday, hoping he would die in the beginning of the week that would ensure a super long holiday.

Singh died on a Sunday, ensuring a long holiday for Government and PSU workers. Many took off for the hills on Friday, December 23, and returned to work on Monday, January 2 - a total of 10 days.

The public sector banks like SBI and PNB were closed the whole time, causing nightmarish scenarios for customers.

The following week, a private bank, probably Citibank or ICICI Bank, brought out a full page ad in all national newspapers, with this message:

Monday: We were open
Tuesday: We were open
Wednesday: We were open
Thursday: We were open
Friday: We were open
Saturday: We were open.

This was one of the iconic ads of that time. Almost everyone went out and opened an account in Citibank, ICICI, HDFC etc.

Today, most Indians from the middle class and high net worth group, use private banking for their day to day needs. Most companies use private banks for salary accounts. Banks like SBI are used primarily for term deposits or by entities that require large amounts of small denomination notes and coins.

Today, if you enter a PSU bank you generally see a bunch of unhappy employees who have to deal with the lower strata of society, pensioners etc. All the valuable customers have moved to private banks where they are treated like VIPs and are usually addressed by their name by the bank staff.

Plus, with net / app banking the norm, most people rarely have to visit a branch.

Private bank employees can be sometimes pushy, trying to get you to buy pension plans, insurance etc. But a polite but firm no is enough to end the spiel.

So when millions of PSU bank employees go on strike on September 28, 29 and 30, most Indians won't feel a thing. Except indifference.
